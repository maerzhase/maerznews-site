Frontier models · Price war

Claude Opus 5.5 takes the intelligence crown — and cuts its own price

Anthropic's new flagship posts the highest score Artificial Analysis has ever measured while listing 20 per cent cheaper than the model it replaces — and OpenAI's GPT-6 Sol answers by undercutting it at half the price.

Ars Technica · Artificial Analysis · reported 22 September

Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5 on 22 September and, on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index, it went straight to the top — a score of 58, the highest the benchmarking outfit says it has measured, and well clear of a field whose median sits at 25. What made the launch unusual was the price tag underneath the score: $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output, down from the $5 and $25 that Opus 5 charged in July. A new flagship that is both more capable and cheaper than the old one breaks the usual pattern, where the best model is also the dearest.

The competitive pressure is easy to read once the numbers are laid out. OpenAI's GPT-6 Sol lists at $2 / $10 — half the Opus 5.5 rate — and on Artificial Analysis's own cost-per-task figures the gap narrows further: Opus 5.5 scored 51.2 at medium effort for about $1.34 a task, against Sol's 47.5 at maximum effort for $1.06, or 39.8 at its default setting for just $0.25. Grok 4.7, from Elon Musk's xAI, sits on 46. The labs are no longer only racing on capability; they are racing on what a unit of that capability costs to run.

The frontier AI model race has entered its comparison-shopping phase. Ars Technica

For anyone building on these models, that shift is the story. A year ago the calculus was which model was smart enough; increasingly it is which one is smart enough at a price that survives contact with a real workload. Anthropic's answer is to fold in cheaper cache reads — $0.20 per million tokens, down from $0.50 — and to keep a pricier fast-mode tier for those who want lower latency; OpenAI's is to lead with a headline rate that looks almost disposable at the default setting.

The comparisons carry an asterisk worth keeping in view. Artificial Analysis has just moved its Intelligence Index to a new version and reweighted the components, so scores from before the change do not line up cleanly with the ones after it, and rival labs quote different leaderboards depending on which cut they favour. The list prices, though, are unambiguous — and they are all pointing the same way: down.

On the EN 327

Ovar signs off on the "rotunda da Pousada"

The Câmara Municipal de Ovar has approved a management agreement with Infraestruturas de Portugal (IP) to build a new roundabout on the EN 327, at the access to Avenida D. Manuel I near the pousada. It is the sort of decision that rarely makes headlines and reshapes a daily commute all the same: the EN 327 is one of the main roads through the concelho, and the junction it fixes is a known pinch-point. Under an "acordo de gestão", the municipality takes on the works on a road that formally belongs to the national network — the mechanism councils increasingly use to get IP-owned junctions built without waiting on Lisbon's own timetable.

After the win

Now the harder question for Die Linke: can it govern?

Two days after coming first in Berlin, Die Linke has moved from the confetti to the awkward part. The party has little recent experience of running a city hall, and the win has revived a row over antisemitism in parts of it. The Neukölln Bundestag member Ferat Koçak is letting his parliamentary functions rest after a member of the Remmo clan turned up at the Neukölln election party, and has apologised for a chat with Remmo's son; an antisemitism researcher, for her part, calls the surrounding debate overblown. The SPD, sunk to its worst-ever Berlin result, is reshuffling too: fraction chief Raed Saleh will not stand again. A Linke–Green–SPD coalition remains the obvious route to the 80 seats a majority needs — but the talks just got more delicate.

Portugal & the Coast

In force today

The "burqa law" takes effect, with fines up to €3,000

From today it is an offence in Portugal to conceal the face in public spaces, under the law popularly known as the "lei das burcas". The statute sets fines of up to €3,000 for covering the face, with carve-outs for health, professional and other justified reasons. It has been contested since it was passed — supporters frame it as a security and integration measure, critics as one aimed at a very small number of people — and its real test now moves to how, and how often, it is actually enforced.
