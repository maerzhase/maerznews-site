Obituary · Software engineering

Margaret Hamilton, who led the code that landed Apollo 11 and named "software engineering", dies at 90

She was the Apollo project's first programmer at MIT and went on to lead more than 400 people. Her idea of software that expects mistakes is now how everyone builds it.

MIT News · BBC · Cambridge, Massachusetts · died 30 September, announced 7 October

Hamilton, born in Paoli, Indiana, in 1936, studied mathematics and came to Boston in 1959 while her husband studied law. A temporary job writing weather-prediction software for the MIT meteorologist Edward Lorenz was her way into programming; her work fed into his later papers on chaos theory. At Lincoln Laboratory she wrote code for SAGE, America's first air-defence system, and began to think about software reliability, then a barely explored idea.

In 1965 her husband spotted a newspaper advert: MIT's Instrumentation Lab wanted people to write software to "send man to the moon". She was hired as the project's first programmer, and its first woman programmer. By 1968 she was assistant director in charge of the Command and Service Module team. When her four-year-old daughter Lauren crashed the simulator by starting the pre-launch program P01 in mid-flight, Hamilton asked for a safeguard and was told astronauts would never do that. On Apollo 8, Jim Lovell did, and her fix went in.

"Looking back, we were the luckiest people in the world; there was no choice but to be pioneers." Margaret Hamilton to MIT News, 2009

In July 1969, moments before Eagle touched down, the guidance computer raised a 1202 alarm: it was overloaded. Her team's priority-driven software dropped background jobs to keep the landing tasks running, and Houston trusted it enough to let Armstrong and Aldrin land. She began calling it "software engineering" to win the work the same respect as hardware; at first, she said, "it was considered to be quite amusing".

She founded Higher Order Software in 1976 and later Hamilton Technologies, wrote more than 130 papers, and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016. The Apollo code went up on GitHub in 2015; Lego made her a minifigure in 2017. A memorial will be held in Cambridge in the spring.

Ovar · High-speed rail

The high-speed line may now take the land it needs between Porto and Oiã

The Diário da República has published the order letting AVAN Norte, the concession company building the Porto–Oiã section of the Lisbon–Porto high-speed line, take administrative possession of the plots on its route. The secretary of state's order declares the land of public utility, the step that allows works to start before compensation is settled. The section runs south from Porto through the Ovar concelho to Oiã, near Oliveira do Bairro. Landowners with questions can visit the line's mobile help centre, which was in Arada on Wednesday and answers questions on the route and on which properties are affected.

Berlin · A100

A steel tendon has snapped inside the Rudolf-Wissell bridge, Berlin's busiest stretch of autobahn

The Autobahn GmbH closed the two outer lanes of the 930-metre bridge that carries the A100 city motorway over the Spree on Wednesday, after first checks found a broken external tendon in one of its hollow box girders. The tendons squeeze the prestressed concrete together so it does not crack under traffic; with one gone, the bridge probably no longer carries its full design load, said the company's Jan-Felix Mühe, though there is no immediate danger because many other tendons hold it. The tendon will be rebuilt over the next weeks, and a static survey is set to last until 21 October. The bridge takes up to 180,000 vehicles a day, on what rbb calls Germany's third-busiest motorway section, but the company expects no extra chaos: only two lanes reach the closed stretch anyway. Spalled concrete reported earlier came from elsewhere on the bridge and is unrelated.

Web · Images

Chrome 155 will display JPEG XL, with a decoder written in Rust

Google is shipping decoding for the .jxl format from Chrome 155, years after pulling an earlier experiment. The Chrome team says JPEG XL compresses 30–50% better than JPEG and adds lossless compression, built-in HDR and lossless re-encoding of existing JPEGs. Because image decoders parse untrusted bytes straight from the network, a classic target for memory bugs, the new one is written in Rust. Google's advice to web developers: try both AVIF and JPEG XL. It expects JPEG XL to win for high-fidelity or lossless photos and where fine-grained progressive loading matters.

Market · Art Basel/UBS

Rich collectors have started asking AI what to buy

The Art Basel & UBS survey, out today, polled 3,100 wealthy collectors in ten markets. In 2024 just 4% used apps or AI tools for advice; now 22% do, about evenly across generations. Gen Z spends most, an average of $347,460 on art in 2025 against $124,265 for all ages, and is also the most private: 39% show their collections only to family and close friends. Painting is still the usual first purchase (43%), and only 1% bought a work over $1 million. Family is the commonest way in, 40% for Gen Z.
