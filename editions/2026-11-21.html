Mathematics · AI · Anthropic

An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude have proved the Riemann hypothesis, in 17.4 million lines of machine-checked code nobody has yet read

Levent Alpöge posted the proof a day after the company's stock market debut. Terence Tao calls it an "enormous" Lean-only proof whose strategy is still being worked out; the Clay Institute's $1 million prize needs two years of scrutiny first.

BBC · The Verge · Terence Tao · Scott Aaronson · as read at 07:00 on 21 November 2026

"I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," Alpöge wrote on his website on Friday. The method, he says, goes further and settles the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and with more time should reach the Extended version too. The hypothesis, posed in 1859, says the zeros of Riemann's zeta function all lie on one line, which amounts to saying the primes are spread as evenly as chance allows. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems; in 25 years only the Poincaré conjecture has been officially solved.

The proof exists, for now, only as computer code in Lean, a language in which a machine checks every logical step. Tao says the best place to follow verification is a Lean community thread, and that a first look suggests it uses "the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry". The BBC says the work took about six weeks on a non-public version of Claude. Alpöge says he messaged Dario Amodei "late Sunday night" and held off until after Thursday's listing: "The precise timing is horrible," he writes, and the cynical reading of it is "false". Anthropic said in August it did not expect Claude's techniques then to lead to a proof, and has not commented.

Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line. Levent Alpöge, in the note announcing the proof

The same day Tao listed two more results with Alpöge's name on them: with Tristan Buckmaster and Matei Coiculescu, a readable, Lean-checked proof that the 3D Euler equations of fluid flow can blow up in finite time, and with Buckmaster a similar result for Navier–Stokes. Scott Aaronson notes that no AI result with a Lean certificate has yet been retracted and bets it will stand, while warning that Lean's own bugs must be fixed before anyone fully trusts such proofs. Why it matters: mathematicians accept a proof when they can explain it, and 17 million lines will take years to explain. Markets were less impressed; see The World.

Ovar · Council

About 250 Ovar council workers found no salary and no Christmas bonus in their accounts on payday, after weeks of assurances that the cyberattack would not touch the payroll

Friday, 20 November, was the day the câmara's staff were due their monthly pay plus the subsídio de Natal. For about 250 of them the money did not arrive. Unofficially, the explanation is a banking failure: the file the council sends each month to pay salaries was not passed on properly to the banks where those workers hold their accounts. Earlier in the week staff had been told that, despite the October cyberattack, no problem with the payroll was expected. OvarNews asked the council what happened and when the money would arrive and had no answer by Friday evening; people inside said efforts were under way to credit it the same day.

The opposition pounced. Emanuel Oliveira, the PS councillor, says he asked the mayor at the last council meeting whether pay and bonus were safe and got a guarantee "without any reservation". "The problem is not admitting there may be difficulties. The problem is guaranteeing there are none when you cannot be sure," he said, aiming at how the uncertainty was handled, not at the staff. Chega's Flávio Costa also questioned the mayor's assurances. Why it matters: a month after the attack, cm-ovar.pt is still dark, and this is the first time it has reached people's bank balances.

Berlin · Budget

Fewer desks than staff: Berlin could save €120–150 million a year by sharing desks and renting no more offices, as it hunts for €5 billion

Since 2019 the target has been at most 15.4 square metres of office per employee, with 1.3 staff per desk. Few departments have managed it: the average across the Senate and districts is still above 21 square metres, says the city's property company BIM. A finance report from July seen by rbb shows the spread. The interior department is at 15.39; the Senate Chancellery uses 32.37; among districts, Charlottenburg-Wilmersdorf takes 26.6 and Mitte 16.51. Top of the table is the Landesdenkmalamt, the monuments office, at 77.54 square metres a head, a generous two-room flat.

Spandau shows how it works. In the youth office 14 staff share ten desks, named after cities like "Copenhagen" and "London" because nobody could remember the numbers, and the freed space became a kitchen for case meetings. By 2031 the district wants to need no rented space, saving about €1.8 million a year. Former finance senator Matthias Kollatz says that applied citywide, giving up rented offices alone is worth €120–150 million a year.

Security · AI routers

Two million booby-trapped prompts show the AI supply chain leaking secrets like a sieve

Many AI apps don't call a model maker directly but go through "AI routers" such as OpenRouter or Vercel, which pass each prompt to whichever inference provider is cheapest or best suited. HD Moore, chief research officer at Dragos, seeded nearly two million prompts with unique, never-used secrets: working AWS keys for empty accounts, SSH passwords, private URLs and git references, each sent to exactly one provider, on infrastructure he controlled.

Then he watched where they surfaced. He found evidence that at least three providers had been compromised by attackers siphoning prompts, resellers who logged prompts and passed them on to people who used the credentials, and several providers ignoring "zero data retention" and "no data collection" flags. Why it matters: if your app or your coding agent pastes a key into a prompt, assume it may end up in a stranger's hands.

Lisbon · Museums

The Museu Nacional de Arte Antiga reopens today after more than a year of works, its decorative arts shown anew

Portugal's national museum of old art in Lisbon was closed for over a year for building works, RTP reports, and reopens to the public this Saturday. Público watched the last days of installation: fitters putting LED lights into vitrines and moving azulejo panels while conservators handled ceramics, porcelain, ivory and silver.

The renewed rooms run, in Público's phrase, "from the altar to the bed": crosses and chalices made for religious rites, a bed made in Japan that is one of a kind, and a seductive Christ. Rooms that were among the quietest in the museum are now meant to be seen again, and as new.
