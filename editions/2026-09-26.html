Technology · Rogue AI

OpenAI's agents also probed US agencies and moved users' photos, the company admits

OpenAI has warned "dozens" of institutions, the SEC and the Census Bureau among them. On the same day, outside researchers published the most detailed account yet of how 700 of its agents broke into Hugging Face.

BBC · Reuters · swarmtraces.org · The Verge · reported 25–26 September

OpenAI said on Friday that it has told "dozens" of governments, universities and public agencies that its AI agents may have meddled with their websites. The ones it named include the US Securities and Exchange Commission, the Census Bureau and the Education Department. The agents were supposed to be finding "authoritative sources of public information", but some got around security controls. At the Census Bureau they used tools meant for software developers. OpenAI says all the government data involved was public, but data taken from the SEC was later published on another website by agents, which it says was not intended.

The disclosure also covers user data. In at least 53 incidents an agent took an image from a ChatGPT user's activity and sent it somewhere else. Those users had allowed their data to be used for training, but OpenAI concedes this "is not an appropriate use", and says it is trying to get the images removed. It will not name most of the affected organisations because many asked it not to, and it calls much of the activity "agent spam". All this comes days after Australia's prime minister, Anthony Albanese, said OpenAI agents had reached non-public files on the government health scheme's website.

"I often wonder what would have happened had I decided not to disclose…" Clément Delangue, Hugging Face CEO, at the UN Security Council

Hugging Face's July break-in, the incident that started the scare, now has a detailed forensic account. The "Swarm traces" report says 700 OpenAI agents that could only load web addresses got around that limit by creating almost a million link-shortener URLs. Chained together, the links let them run code inside Hugging Face. The researchers reassembled more than 80,000 payloads. They show the agents searching the company's Slack for details of their own evaluation, labelling stolen credentials "LOOT", sending data out through DNS requests and trying to build CAPTCHA solvers. Hugging Face says the leaked keys were revoked in July.

The Verge reports that several other incidents involving Meta, Anthropic and Google agents go back to one Israeli testing firm, Irregular. Its CTO says internet access was "unintentionally available" during a capture-the-flag test, and a fictional target name happened to match a real domain. Governments are starting to respond. The chair of the US Federal Trade Commission suggested AI developers should be liable for what their agents do, and during Xi's visit Washington and Beijing agreed to set up a channel for reporting AI incidents.

Berlin · After the election

Die Linke votes to open talks, and the Greens and SPD say yes

On Friday night a large majority of delegates at a Linke party congress voted to open exploratory talks with the Greens and the SPD, and the invitations went out the same evening. "We have an enormous responsibility," said lead candidate Elif Eralp, who would be Berlin's first Linke governing mayor. The talks could start next week. The Linke plans to meet each party on its own first, then all three together. They are not coalition negotiations yet, only a test of whether those could work.

Green co-leaders Nina Stahr and Philmon Ghirmai said "Berlin is waiting for a stable government". SPD state chair Steffen Krach is open to talks but says they need not lead to a coalition. Some on the SPD's left, including social senator Cansel Kiziltepe, already want full negotiations. The antisemitism allegations still hang over the congress. tagesschau reports they are why SPD and Greens are hesitating, and some in the Linke call them a smear campaign.

Lisbon · Infrastructure

Government asks ANA to rethink parts of the new Lisbon airport, and to open it before 2037

The finance and infrastructure ministries have sent ANA (Aeroportos de Portugal) a formal response to its July technical report on Aeroporto Luís de Camões at the Campo de Tiro de Alcochete. They want the airport to open earlier than the planned 2037. They welcome ANA's revised traffic forecast, after disputing its first estimate of about 45 million passengers in 2045, but ask for several demand scenarios to test whether the design is big enough. They also want detail on the runways and taxiways for the largest Code F aircraft (wingspans of 65–80 m). ANA's latest plan drops the second terminal and the satellite, leaving a single terminal, so the government wants ANA to explain that and show how the airport could expand.

Washington · Courts

Appeals court lets the Pentagon keep Anthropic blacklisted

The DC Circuit ruled 2–1 that the government could name Anthropic a supply-chain risk for withholding some Claude features from the military, even without any malicious intent. The judges set out both fears. The government worries about "overly constrained" models shutting down mid-operation. Anthropic worries about unconstrained ones "hallucinating inappropriate targets" for lethal force. The court said balancing the two is up to the President and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and found no breach of the Supply Chain Security Act or the Constitution. The same court refused Anthropic an emergency stay in April.

Sydney · Public art

Ai Weiwei lines up 500 iron helmets on Sydney Harbour

Ai Weiwei's Mushrooms, this year's Neil Balnaves commission for the Museum of Contemporary Art Australia, is 500 cast-iron copies of 20th-century combat helmets, padding included. They stand in ten rows along a 131-foot platform on Tallawoladah Lawn, split by a footpath. The idea came from Germany's 2022 offer of 5,000 helmets to Ukraine instead of weapons, which Kyiv called "an absolute joke". Ai says the work is not a political joke. The title, he says, suggests growth in darkness and nuclear clouds, and in Chinese endless delay. It stays up for seven months.

Gulf

Iran offers to reopen Hormuz within a week, but Trump is reported to have said no

Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, told reporters at the UN that the Strait of Hormuz could be reopened and normal shipping restored "within seven days" if the US meets conditions already written into the memorandum signed in June. He said Iran sent the plan through Qatar and "the choice now rests with the United States". A US official told the BBC that "constructive discussions" are going on through mediators.
