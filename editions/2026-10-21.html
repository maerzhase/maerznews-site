Berlin · Cyberattack

Berlin's hacked Senate: the transport senator's ID card is on the darknet, and attack detection covered 5% of devices

rbb24 went through the files the Rhysida gang published after its attack on two Senate departments and found Ute Bonde's identity card, her stored signature and her predecessors' mail. The system meant to spot such intrusions had licences for one machine in twenty, and neither the entry point nor the leaking servers were among them.

rbb24 Recherche · Berlin, 21 October 2026

A copy of the identity card of Ute Bonde (CDU), Berlin's senator for transport, climate and the environment, a stored copy of her digital signature, and e-mails of her predecessors Manja Schreiner (CDU) and Bettina Jarasch (Greens) are all in the data that the ransomware group Rhysida published on the darknet after breaking into two Senate departments. rbb's analysis found many more ID copies and personal papers of ordinary staff in the dump. The transport department confirmed Bonde is affected and described the haul as mostly "unstructured data from shared and personal directories".

The more damaging finding is about defence. The system for detecting cyber attacks introduced in 2026 had licences for only 5% of all devices, the urban-development department told rbb; extending it was impossible "for financial reasons". Neither the computer identified as the attackers' way in nor the servers the data later flowed from were covered. In 2024, money for IT security was available "only partly to the necessary extent", although a state guideline has required adequate staff and funds since 2017, and Berlin had planned in 2023 to expand exactly these detection systems.

"They accepted the security risk with their eyes open." Stefan Ziller, Greens digital spokesman, to rbb

The federal BSI had warned at least since March 2025 about "ClickFix", in which a fake captcha gets users to run a command that loads malware, and in September described a variant, "TerminalFix", used to reach servers and backups; it ties malware used in such attacks to the group behind Rhysida. It will not say whether Berlin was hit that way, and neither the state IT provider ITDZ nor the Senate Chancellery will say whether the warnings ever reached the two departments. Jarasch, told only informally that she was affected, was informed that a formal data request would take months because of the volume of requests; she calls the state's handling "disastrous". Ziller wants Berlin's IT security centralised.

Ovar · Cyberattack

Ovar council now warns residents' personal data may have been taken in the weekend cyberattack

In a new statement on Tuesday evening, the Câmara said that "given the breadth of the systems affected", personal data "of various kinds" handled by municipal services may be involved. It has not said which data, nor whether any misuse has been confirmed, but lists the risks as fraud, phishing, identity theft and other unlawful use of personal information.

Anyone who deals with the council should be wary of unsolicited messages that use their details or claim to come from the Município de Ovar, and should never hand over personal data, passwords or bank details without first checking the contact is genuine. The council says containment measures started as soon as the attack was confirmed and that investigation and recovery are under way; on Monday it had said counters, e-mail and phones would be down until today.

Security · Bundeswehr

An old explosive drone near Wunstorf air base leads the Bundeswehr to rate the Russian sabotage threat "considerable" for the first time

An internal security assessment seen by WDR and the Süddeutsche Zeitung raises the danger of Russian sabotage against German troops to "erheblich", the second-highest level on the scale. For the first time the armed forces assume they themselves can be the target of Russian attacks inside Germany, citing Russia's "almost certain" capability and now "very probably" heightened intent.

The trigger was a farmer's find in Lower Saxony in mid-September: a crashed drone close to the Wunstorf airbase, built much like the explosives-laden drone used in the failed attack on Leipzig/Halle airport in early August, with a tin of high explosive lying a few metres away. The BKA's analysis suggests it had lain there for about two years, and investigators believe that, unlike in Leipzig, the target was not civilian cargo planes but possibly the Bundeswehr's own aircraft.

A day after the tents came down, housing protests spread to five more cities, and Lisbon's camp is back in the Rossio today

Tuesday evening's rallies drew thousands in Lisbon and Porto and spread well beyond them, in solidarity with the two camps the PSP cleared at dawn: about 400 people in Coimbra, two hundred in Braga, more than a hundred in Évora, around 50 in Leiria, with gatherings in Beja and Faro too. The Lisbon movement says it will pitch its tents in the Rossio again on Wednesday, defying interior minister Luís Neves, who declared the "acampadas" over and warned the police would act again. Porto's organisers have called a two-day break; by night Praça D. João I was almost empty. People detained in Lisbon were due before a judge this morning, with a vigil called outside the Campus de Justiça, and the PSD and Chega both say they are open to negotiating on housing.

Security · Web PKI

Eight CA keys so weak one of them was "4": eMudhra's intermediates could have minted a certificate for any site

The eight intermediate certificates belong to India's eMudhra. Their private keys were trivial to guess, heise reports, so anyone could have issued browser-trusted TLS certificates for any domain. The alarm came from a site, emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com, posting "We have a problem here - Wayne", signed with one of the keys as proof.

By Tuesday morning browser makers and several major apps and programming languages had blocked all eight, and certificate transparency logs showed no malicious issuance. The harder part is everything else that ships its own trust store: Linux distributions, embedded and IoT devices will take far longer to update.

Canada · Referendum

Alberta votes by more than two to one to stay in Canada, handing Carney a mandate against Trump

Preliminary results of Monday's separation referendum show more than two-thirds of Albertans want to remain in Canada. Prime Minister Mark Carney, who grew up in the province, had told crowds in Calgary to "vote like Canada depends on it"; afterwards he said Albertans "chose unity, to build together". The Forever Canadian campaign of former provincial minister Thomas Lukaszuk played a key part.

The province holds most of Canada's proven oil reserves and is central to Ottawa's plan to survive the US trade war by finding new markets, the BBC's Lyse Doucet writes. Trump is meanwhile being sued over his tariffs on Canada in the latest legal challenge to them.
