Technology · Software engineering

AI coding agents write a third more code, and the software that ships doesn't grow

A Harvard study of more than 700 software firms finds the extra code piling up in review: pull requests take 49% longer to merge, while the features and issues teams close stay flat.

Ars Technica · Fiona Chen & James Stratton, Harvard University · Jellyfish data, 2021 to March 2026

The headline numbers look like a productivity boom. When a firm introduces AI coding agents, the tools that write and submit code on their own from a prompt, its engineers produce 30% more lines of code, 20% more commits and 23% more pull requests on average. That is what Harvard researchers Fiona Chen and James Stratton found in aggregated data from Jellyfish, an engineering-analytics company: 300 million "work events" such as commits and pull requests across more than 700,000 employees at over 700 software firms, from 2021 to March 2026, compared before and after each firm adopted assistants or agents.

But the work the business actually tracks did not move. The rate at which issues and epics, whole features in Jira and similar tools, were resolved showed no statistically significant change after AI tools arrived, and the issues did not get bigger or more complex either. The authors find "little evidence that firms increase software output or reduce employment"; the gains in writing code are "absorbed by downstream constraints in the production process".

"The code review process significantly increases in length, pull requests are more likely to require revisions, and reviewers leave more comments." Chen & Stratton, Harvard University

The constraint is human review. The time from a pull request being opened to being merged grows 49% on average once agents are in use. The share of pull requests sent back with changes requested nearly doubles, and comments per pull request rise 35%. Firms respond by putting more people on it: the share of workers doing code reviews rises 14%. Headcount, cross-checked against LinkedIn, shows no change the authors can attribute to AI.

Machines are not yet picking up the slack. By March 2026, 80% of the firms used some form of AI code review, and 95% had adopted coding agents, yet agents accounted for only 23.3% of review comments and 10.8% of pull requests. The data stop before the latest generation of agents, and teams are still learning where they help. For anyone running a TypeScript codebase the lesson is practical: an agent that doubles your diffs without shrinking your reviewers' queue has moved the work, not removed it.

Aveiro · Águeda · Roads

Bids for the €109 million Aveiro–Águeda road get five more weeks, but no money is in the 2027 budget

Bids for the new road between Aveiro and Águeda were due last Wednesday. The two councils, which are tendering it jointly, have pushed the deadline to 16 November, with bids opened the next day, after several companies asked for clarifications. Águeda's council says several Portuguese and foreign consortia are interested, some already registered on the tender platform and some already meeting about land for building sites. The base price is €109.3 million, for 950 days of works. The government has made the road part of the national network and promised money from the recovery fund (PRR) and then the state budget, but its 2027 budget, presented on Thursday, has nothing for it next year. Infrastructure minister Miguel Pinto Luz said this year the road is "for real"; Aveiro's mayor Luís Souto says he still trusts the government's commitments, to the road and to the enlargement of Aveiro's hospital.

Berlin · Coalition talks

Berlin's left-wing coalition talks spill into the federal government

The Left, Greens and SPD in Berlin are about to begin exploratory talks, and the row has now reached Friedrich Merz's coalition. Culture minister Wolfram Weimer, who has no party but is close to the CDU, told Bild that if SPD leaders Lars Klingbeil and Bärbel Bas do not "immediately and unambiguously" stop a left-wing alliance in the capital, it would amount to "a vote of no confidence in our coalition". He accused Islamist and far-left milieus of joining forces in Berlin and said such a government would directly affect federal culture policy. SPD general secretary Tim Klüssendorf hit back that anyone speculating about the federal coalition "has not understood how serious the situation is", and told Weimer to worry about whether the CDU will keep ruling out cooperation with the far right in Saxony-Anhalt, where the AfD wants to name the premier and CDU deputies might help it. The doubts are not only on the right: former Green leader Omid Nouripour called Berlin's Left "a security risk, not only for Berlin", citing antisemitic incidents, and there is resistance within the Left itself to the agreement its negotiators signed with the Greens and SPD.

Porto · Lisbon · Aveiro · Protest

"Não dá mais para viver assim": thousands march in Lisbon, Porto, Aveiro and Braga

A platform of more than 30 organisations, from unions to housing, anti-racism and climate groups, brought people onto the streets of four cities on Saturday under the slogan "We can't go on living like this". In Lisbon several thousand gathered in Largo do Intendente and marched down Avenida Almirante Reis; in Porto about 1,500, by the PSP's count, filled the square outside the Rivoli, and by 19:00 more than 20 tents stood in Praça de D. João I. Their demands: price controls on essentials and fuel, a tax on the windfall profits of banks, energy companies and supermarkets, and action on housing. Many placards remembered Maricarmen Abascal, the 87-year-old evicted in Spain whose case set off protests there. The same day, the labour minister ruled out, for now, a minimum wage above €970, and finance minister Joaquim Miranda Sarmento again rejected zero VAT on basic goods and said he saw "no reason" for protests over higher university fees.

A hidden implant in resold Ledger wallets may explain $86 million in crypto thefts

Reports of drained accounts among Ledger users have been mounting, and they appear to trace back to hardware sold by one reseller, CryptoBillis. Germany · Prize

Gabriele Stötzer, the GDR's unruly body artist, receives the Kaiserring

Goslar's Kaiserring, awarded every year since 1975 and one of the most prestigious prizes for modern art, has gone to an East German woman for the first time. Gabriele Stötzer of Erfurt receives it for her photographs, films and performances of the body, made in the GDR's underground; the jury calls her "one of the central figures of the GDR art scene". Jailed in the 1970s after protesting Wolf Biermann's expulsion, she stayed in the country anyway and in 1989 was among those who occupied the Stasi's Erfurt headquarters. Her largest institutional show yet, "Dabei sein und nicht schweigen" (being there and not keeping quiet), is at Berlin's Gropius Bau until 6 December.

"Time for Ukraine to get a new president," says Trump, after Zelensky attacks his diesel deal

A day after confirming that US sanctions on Russian diesel exports are suspended until 7 April, Donald Trump told reporters outside the White House that Volodymyr Zelensky "wants to make problems for the world" by ordering strikes on Russian refineries, and that Ukraine should "get a new leader who can make a deal".
