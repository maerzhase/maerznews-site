Markets · AI · After the midterms

The AI boom meets the ballot box: chip stocks have their worst day since the pandemic as voters turn on data centres

The Nasdaq lost 5.5% and the US chip index more than 12% on Thursday; Tokyo, Seoul and Taipei followed this morning. Investors now expect a Democratic Congress, and Democratic governors, to slow the building of the data centres AI runs on.

BBC · heise online · tagesschau · 5–6 November 2026

The markets shrugged on Wednesday, the day after the midterms; Frankfurt's DAX even touched a record early on Thursday. Then the mood turned. By the New York close the Nasdaq was down 5.5% and the S&P 500 4.4%, their worst day since 2025, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, the 30 biggest chip companies, had lost more than 12%, a fall last seen at the start of the pandemic. SanDisk dropped 22.3%, Micron 17.5%, AMD 17.3%, Nvidia 9.3%. In Europe, Infineon lost more than 8.5% and ASML 6.5%.

The reason is on the ballot papers. Democrats ran on slowing AI down, and dozens of local votes on new data centres went against them, all but one, in Harwood, North Dakota, heise reports. Texas's governor-elect Gina Hinojosa has promised a moratorium on new construction; Bernie Sanders called on X for a national one. With both houses of Congress and a string of governor's offices now in Democratic hands, investors are pricing in a slower build-out, on top of older doubts about whether the vast AI spending will ever pay off.

"The Stock Market crashed today because the Dumocrats had a big Tuesday — So sad to see, you only have yourselves to blame!" Donald Trump, on Truth Social

Asia opened lower on Friday. The Nikkei was 6.3% down in the afternoon, with the memory maker Kioxia off 11.5%; Seoul's Kospi fell more than 8% in the morning and set off a circuit breaker that briefly halted trading; Taiwan's Taiex is heading for its worst week since 2001. The rout reaches the AI companies themselves: the Wall Street Journal reports that big investors now doubt Anthropic can float at the $1.5 trillion once hoped for, with one putting it nearer $1 trillion.

Santa Maria da Feira · Cyberattack

Feira's council is knocked offline by a cyberattack overnight, three weeks after the one that hit Ovar

Santa Maria da Feira's town hall says it was the target of "a computer crime" during the night into Thursday, 5 November. All of its IT systems are affected, and its email addresses and landline numbers are down too. The national cybersecurity centre (CNCS) has been told, technicians are working out how far the attack reached, and the council will post updates on its social media while its own channels are dark.

"They may try to disrupt our work, but they will not shake our determination," said the mayor, Amadeu Albergaria. Feira is the concelho on Ovar's eastern side, and the pattern is familiar here: Ovar's council was hit on 18 October, its website is still offline, and only this week did it get its email working again. Whether the two attacks are linked has not been said.

Berlin · Youth welfare

One post in ten is empty at Berlin's youth offices, and each caseworker carries 30 families against a recommended 27

Berlin's Jugendämter, the district offices that step in when children are at risk, had 421 of their 4,180 full-time posts unfilled at the end of last year, the education and youth senate department says in an answer to the SPD. The gap varies wildly by district: Friedrichshain-Kreuzberg reported just 1.5% vacant, Steglitz-Zehlendorf almost 17%.

The workload shows it. A caseworker in the regional social services handles about 30 cases a month on average across the city, against a recommended 27; in Pankow it is more than 35, in Mitte and Steglitz-Zehlendorf just over 33. The number of formal overload notices, staff warning their managers they can no longer do the job properly, has been rising for years, rbb24 reports, and it rose again last year. The new red-green-red coalition, now negotiating its contract, inherits the problem.

Lisbon · Housing

The President's housing truce lasts two days: Montenegro meets the PS and Chega, and nobody agrees on anything

Answering President Seguro's call for a deal on housing, the prime minister received the PS leader José Luís Carneiro and Chega's André Ventura in São Bento on Thursday. Carneiro left after an hour and 45 minutes saying there was no agreement on the two points that mattered to him, writing a new rent law "from zero" and defining a "moderate rent", and accused the government of "intransigence". No further meetings are planned.

The rent changes the PS objects to were approved in principle on 30 September with the AD, the IL and an abstaining Chega, after a PSD–Chega pact on amendments. That pact now looks dead too, Observador reports: Ventura complained of the PS's "lack of shame", the AD side spoke of childishness, and its sources say Carneiro "has two faces". Meanwhile the protest camps that started it all are in their fourth week.

Linux · Security

"*sshole": type more than 256 characters and walk into some Alpine Linux machines over SSH, no password needed

A decade-old flaw sits where OpenSSH meets musl, the slim C library Alpine uses instead of glibc. It bites when sshd runs without PAM, at least one account has a SHA-256 or SHA-512 password hash, and the target account is passwordless, marked "*" in /etc/shadow, as accounts meant for key-based login often are. "It takes one connection, works every time, and needs no retries," Alpine's maintainers say.

OpenSSH and Alpine shipped updates on Thursday, while each side says the root cause lies with the other. The reach is the worry: Alpine's Docker base image has passed a billion pulls, and it runs inside routers, TVs and industrial controllers that may never be patched. Shodan lists about 22 million machines with port 22 open.

Paraguay · Politics

Paraguay's president resigns over a collapsed bank, and Congress refuses to let him go so it can impeach him instead

Santiago Peña announced his resignation in a video on Thursday, apologising "if at any point I failed". The crisis began when Ueno Bank went into insolvency and froze all its operations; lawmakers accuse Peña, a shareholder until last year in the company that owned it, of conflicts of interest and misconduct, which he denies.

In Paraguay Congress must accept a president's resignation. It did not: a special session adjourned in favour of the impeachment the lower house had just voted to pursue, and prosecutors have opened an inquiry. Peña is due to present his defence on Friday, as police and protesters clashed outside the Congress building.
