Germany · Transport

The Deutschlandticket is set to cost €66.90 from 2027, its third rise in three years

Bund, Länder and the transport industry decide today. If the reported figure holds, the ticket that launched at €49 will cost over a third more than when it began.

tagesschau · Rheinische Post · 30 September

The Deutschlandticket, which covers local and regional buses and trains across Germany, will probably go up again next year, to €66.90 a month. The Rheinische Post reports the figure from the ticket's coordinating council, where the federal government, the states, local authorities and the transport industry sit together. The council is due to settle the price today. It would be the third increase: the ticket started at €49, went to €58 in 2025 and to €63 this year.

The price is now worked out from a cost index that includes staff, energy and running costs. About 14.5 million people use the ticket. The federal government and the states each put in €1.5 billion a year to limit the operators' losses. For a subscriber on a monthly plan, the rise would come to €3.90 a month, or €46.80 a year.

"The constant arguments about the ticket and its price are tiring and create uncertainty." Jens Hilgenberg, BUND

The environmental group BUND says it is unacceptable that the government found €2.5 billion of taxpayers' money for another fuel discount but cannot keep the ticket's price stable. People need to be able to plan for years ahead, said its transport head Jens Hilgenberg, if they are to change how they travel. Detlef Neuß, honorary chair of the passenger group Pro Bahn, said "that's enough for now". His group accepts the rise only "through gritted teeth", and he too pointed at the fuel discount: ticket holders should get a discount as well.

The transport operators' association VDV urged the council to agree today. Operators have to plan for the new price and tell customers in time.

Estarreja · Industry

Tensai puts €15.9 million into robots and a bigger furniture plant in Estarreja

Tensai de Portugal showed off the progress of its "Productive Innovation Project" at the Estarreja factory on Tuesday and announced a new round of investment. The project costs about €15.92 million, of which €15.33 million qualifies for support under the PT2030 and COMPETE2030 programmes, with about €5.36 million coming from the European Regional Development Fund. The main piece is a new industrial building to expand plastic furniture production, where the plant now makes about 350,000 pieces a year for hotels, restaurants, homes and gardens. Robots will take over key steps, and a manufacturing execution system will link production planning, machines and factory data in real time, alongside digital printing and digital logistics.

Berlin · Housing

Berlin has two million square metres of empty offices. They might make a few thousand flats

In 2019 only 1.8% of Berlin's offices stood empty, a record low. Now it is 8.6%, almost two million square metres, says Helge Scheunemann of the property firm JLL. Flats, by contrast, are only 1 to 2% vacant, and the city is short of more than 100,000. Opponents of socialising big landlords such as Vonovia say converting offices is the better answer. The economist Kritikos, one of them, reckons a third of the empty space, 600,000 to 700,000 m², converts easily: "perhaps 10,000 flats", a tenth of the need. "No, that's not enough," he says himself.

Scheunemann thinks even that is optimistic: only whole empty buildings can be converted, leaving "just a few thousand". Owners hold out because conversion forces them to write down values he thinks are 25 to 30% too high. Offices have long corridors, one kitchen per floor and deep, dark floor plans; HTW architect Regina Zeitner warns of costly drilling and asbestos under the carpet, and says a conversion "can easily take three years". KfW covers 30% of costs, up to €30,000 a flat but €300,000 per applicant. Kritikos would go further: a levy on long-empty offices, then orders to build.

Wildfires · Season's end

The fire season's peak ends with more fires but 72% less land burned

Portugal's most critical firefighting period ends today. Nearly 8,000 rural fires broke out this year, more than last year, but they burned 74,267 hectares, 72% less than in the same period of 2025. Since 1 July the "delta" level kept the full force on the ground: 15,149 firefighters in 2,595 teams, 3,463 vehicles, 50 bulldozers and 81 aircraft, three of them helicopters of the forestry firm AFOCELCA. From tomorrow fewer crews, vehicles and aircraft stay on duty.

AI · White House

Trump gets AI bosses to sign a "morally binding" pledge to police themselves

At what he called a "Super Intelligence" summit on Tuesday, Trump and the heads of Google, Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, SpaceX and Nvidia signed an accord that leaves the companies responsible for their own models' safety. They promise safeguards to keep models working as intended, quick fixes when they don't, and "independent auditors" to check that systems do not "hack or access technical systems in unintended ways", the week's recurring fear. Trump called it "almost like a constitution" and said he would set up a board to oversee AI safety, without saying who would sit on it. He rules out legislation. An NTU governance researcher said the pact does not settle who is liable when things go wrong.

Painting · Politics

Kehinde Wiley asks Trump to sell him back the painting the embassy took down

In August the State Department removed Wiley's "Young Artists After Siamesas 1960" (2015) from the US embassy in Santo Domingo, citing the artist's "violent and racist words and depictions"; officials had called it "woke". It was commissioned for the embassy in 2013. The department said lawyers were checking whether the work could be sold. In a letter to Trump dated 24 September, Wiley asks for the chance to buy it: "I created this painting for a particular place and with a particular purpose." He spent two years studying Dominican artists and made it with young painters in Santo Domingo.

Noise rock · Gilla Band's first album in four years

France · Schools

440 detained, mostly minors, as France's school protests turn violent

Pupils have blockaded high schools for days over overcrowded classes and teacher shortages, and on Tuesday some joined civil servants marching against the government's spending cuts. Interior minister Laurent Nuñez said 440 people were detained, two-thirds of them held in custody. At least 400 schools were affected; 10 teachers, several pupils and 48 police officers were hurt as bins burned and bus stops were smashed. "There are 34 of us in each class," a 16-year-old told AFP. "The teachers are overworked." The pupils' union accuses the police of excessive force.
