AI · Stock market · Prospectus

Anthropic files for what could be the biggest share sale ever: revenue up fourteenfold to $16.3 billion, and some $550 billion promised for chips and cloud

The maker of Claude published its prospectus on Monday and wants to list on Nasdaq as ANT, possibly in November. The filing shows a business that now makes an operating profit, a paper loss of $80.7 billion, founders who keep control, and risk factors that include the chance its own models turn out to be sentient.

heise online · The Verge · BBC · Reuters · Anthropic S-1 · 26–27 October 2026

Anthropic had filed confidentially in June; on Monday the 300-page document went public. Neither the number of shares nor a price is set. Bloomberg says the listing could come in November and beat SpaceX's record of about $86 billion raised in June; the New York Times estimates more than $100 billion of shares at a valuation of up to $2 trillion. Asian markets rose on the news overnight, Reuters reports.

The growth is steep: revenue of $16.3 billion in the first six months, about 80% of it from usage fees, mostly businesses paying for API access and coding tools, and 20% from subscriptions. The operating loss shrank from $2.1 billion to $693 million, and operating cash flow turned positive at $3.9 billion. The $80.7 billion net loss is mostly accounting: as the company's value rose, so did the book value of certain investor claims, a $72.4 billion charge with no cash leaving the building.

AI systems "may behave in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways" that could damage the company. Anthropic's S-1, risk factors

The costs are what the chart shows. Compute and infrastructure spending rose from $2.3 to $8.9 billion in the half, and the contracts stretch years ahead: Broadcom chip leases of $161.2 billion, up to $3.1 billion a month to SpaceX for Nvidia GPUs, and cloud deals with Google, Amazon and Microsoft. Through a "Founder LLC", the seven co-founders will hold 50.1% of the vote on key matters. The risk section warns of "catastrophic" harm beyond what "the insurance industry in the aggregate" could cover, and names only two direct rivals: OpenAI and Google. Anthropic makes Claude, the model that writes this paper.

FlixBus stops in Ovar: a new line runs to Porto, Coimbra, Santarém and Évora, from opposite the station

The long-distance coach operator has added Ovar to its Portuguese network with a line linking the town to Porto, Coimbra, Santarém and Évora. Industry · Politics

Audi's chief thanks Trump on a campaign stage for a new Georgia plant, and Germany asks what happens to Neckarsulm

On Saturday Donald Trump announced, at a Republican rally in Perry, Georgia, that Audi would build its first US factory there; chief executive Gernot Döllner then stepped up and thanked him. The plant, worth about $4 billion (€3.6 billion), is to start construction in 2027 and from the early 2030s make 200,000 large SUVs a year, the Q7, Q8 and Q9 now built in Bratislava, with 4,500 jobs.

The backlash has not stopped. The worry is concrete in Neckarsulm, Audi's first headquarters: A5 and A6 production ends in 2033 and no successor model is promised. Audi says it was asked to present the plans with the president, takes the "political signal" seriously and remains party-politically neutral.

AI · Mathematics

OpenAI says its models have refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis, and the people who proposed it will check

The paper, posted to GitHub with a Lean proof, gives a deterministic algorithm that counts the satisfying assignments of fixed-width CNF formulas in subexponential time and polynomial space. Applied to 3-SAT, that would make false the 1999 conjecture, widely believed and underpinning many hardness results, that such problems need exponential time.

A group led by Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi, who proposed ETH, and MIT's Ryan Williams will examine it. OpenAI also claims a proof of Sogge's local smoothing conjecture for waves and an existence proof for quantum field theories including QCD, to be vetted by a group under David Gross. A panel of mathematicians had just advised labs against giving chosen experts early access, and asked for repository releases with formal proofs instead.

Lisbon · Film heritage

Lost since its premiere, "A Lenda de Miragaia" (1931) was found in two cans at the Feira da Ladra, and screens tonight

Silhouette animation short · Portugal · 28,400 frames · Cinemateca, 27 Oct

One of Portuguese cinema's first animated films, by Raúl Faria da Fonseca and António Cunhal, brother of the communist leader Álvaro Cunhal, had been given up for lost. In May the cinephile Duarte Veloso and his father spotted two film cans labelled with its title on sale at Lisbon's flea market, paid €25, and gave the nitrate negative to the Cinemateca.

Shot frame by frame with a camera obscura, it brings the Porto legend of Miragaia to the screen in cut-out silhouettes, the technique of "The Adventures of Prince Achmed". The Cinemateca restored it and rebuilt the intertitles and edit from notes on the negative and Almeida Garrett's version of the tale. It is shown today, World Day for Audiovisual Heritage, for the first time since 1931.

Antiquity · Glass

Sheffield scientists remake the Roman glass that turns from green to red

The British Museum's 1,600-year-old Lycurgus Cup is opaque green in ordinary light and glows red when lit from behind, a puzzle for centuries. Researchers at the University of Sheffield have reproduced the effect by embedding gold and silver nanoparticles, "a thousand times smaller than a grain of sand", into glass.

Light passing through is stripped of blue and green, leaving reds and oranges; light bouncing off is scattered into green. "To me, it's magic," technician Lisa Hollands told the Guardian. The cup shows Dionysus triumphing over the vine-bound King Lycurgus.

Algeria · Niger · Sahel

Algeria sends troops abroad for the first time since 1973, to guard energy sites in Niger

Algeria's senate voted 148 of 174 in an emergency session on Sunday, and the lower house followed, to send soldiers to northern Niger, near the Libyan border. It is the first use of a 2020 constitutional clause allowing deployments abroad, and a break with a long refusal to send forces beyond its borders; the last time was to Egypt in the 1973 Arab-Israeli war.

"We are not sending our soldiers to wage war in Niger," one senator said, but to secure economic infrastructure: a planned pipeline to carry Nigerian gas through Niger to Algeria, and Sonatrach's oil exploration in Kafra, where two wells are put at 168 and 100 million barrels. The neighbours are mending a 2025 rift; Niger faces armed groups linked to al-Qaeda and Islamic State.
