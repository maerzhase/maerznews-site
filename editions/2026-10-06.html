Berlin · Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine

A Berlin algae researcher shares the Nobel Prize for switching brain cells on with light

Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University, Georg Nagel of Würzburg and Karl Deisseroth of Stanford share the 2026 prize for optogenetics. It began with a single-celled alga and how it finds the light.

Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet · tagesschau · rbb24 · Ars Technica · 5 October

The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet gave this year's prize in physiology or medicine to Karl Deisseroth (born 1971) of Stanford University and the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, Peter Hegemann (born 1954) of Humboldt University of Berlin, and Georg Nagel (born 1953) of the University of Würzburg, "for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics". The 12 million Swedish kronor are shared equally. Hegemann and Nagel did the prize-winning work at Max Planck institutes in Martinsried and Frankfurt.

It started with Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, a single-celled green alga that swims towards light. When its gene sequences became public around 2000, Hegemann's group found genes resembling known light-sensing proteins. Nagel put the proteins into frog eggs, and together they showed that one, channelrhodopsin-2, is an ion channel opened directly by light. When blue light hits it, charged particles flow into the cell and create an electrical signal, whatever cell it sits in.

"These algae occur here in Brandenburg's lakes, by the way." Peter Hegemann to rbb24

Deisseroth put the gene into rat nerve cells and fired them with short pulses of light, published in 2005. Two years later the switch worked in the brains of living mice. Researchers can now turn chosen neurons on or off and watch what changes, testing whether a group of cells actually causes a movement, a feeling or a memory. Francis Crick had suggested light for exactly this in the late 1970s.

The first medical use is in the eye. Hegemann says a first patient who regained a simple kind of sight was presented in 2021. In September the US FDA accepted for review a gene therapy for retinitis pigmentosa with severe sight loss; it is not yet approved. Hegemann learned of the prize from an email from the committee chair asking him to call. "Of course I was overwhelmed," he told rbb24. He has also said: "You have to take risks."

Esmoriz · Barrinha

The Barrinha opens to the sea and leaves hundreds of fish on Esmoriz beach

Hundreds of small fish lay on the sand at Praia de Esmoriz, next to the Barrinha, on Monday after the lagoon's channel to the sea was opened. Locals say they are pumpkinseed sunfish (perca-sol, Lepomis gibbosus), an invasive species from North America that thrives in calm fresh water. The Barrinha de Esmoriz, or Lagoa de Paramos, lies between Ovar and Espinho and connects to the ocean only from time to time. When the bar opens, the lagoon empties into the sea in a strong surge, and OvarNews says that current probably carried the fish out to the river mouth and onto the beach. Walkers also found many sea hares, known locally as "vinagreiras", on the sand. The lagoon is a Natura 2000 site because of the range of species it holds.

Military service · Figures

Requests to refuse military service have tripled since the new Wehrdienst law

Between January and August, 12,102 people applied to be recognised as conscientious objectors, the federal office for civil-society tasks (BAFzA) told the RND newspapers. That is already three times as many as in the whole of 2025, when there were 3,879, and more than five times the 2,249 of 2024. At the end of June the count stood at 5,862, so it has doubled in two months. Conscription is still suspended, but the new military service law took effect on 1 January and makes medical screening compulsory for young men born in 2008 or later. If too few volunteers come forward, the Bundestag can vote for "conscription on demand". Applications come from reservists and serving soldiers as well as from people who have never served, and counselling centres report rising demand. The right to refuse is in Article 4 of the Basic Law and applies whether or not conscription is active; it needs a written, personal justification.

Industry · Tramagal

Mitsubishi Fuso moves its European headquarters to Portugal

The Japanese truck maker will run imports, sales, after-sales and marketing for 41 European markets from Portugal, along with a European parts distribution centre and development of its Canter and electric eCanter trucks. The new centre replaces the one Daimler Truck ran and will employ 40 people, about 30 of them in new jobs. Its base is the Tramagal plant near Abrantes, for 46 years Fuso's only factory in Europe. It opened in 1964 building French Berliet trucks under licence and has made Mitsubishi vehicles since 1980. In April Fuso merged with Toyota's Hino into Archion, in which Daimler Truck and Toyota hold 25% each. The plant restarted production in September with 400 workers after a summer lay-off.

AI agents · Security

One gullible AI agent can pass an attack to every agent it talks to

In the past five months Google and four other organisations have acknowledged a flaw in how their AI agents talk to each other. Independent researcher Syed Anas Mohiuddin tested agents at Google, JPMorgan Chase, Weaviate, Rapid7, the French government's digital directorate and the US federal government. His proof-of-concept attacks use prompt injection against one special-purpose agent, say for translation or data analysis, rather than against the language model. Such agents often have weak guardrails or none, and they pass the instructions down the chain. Because agents connected over MCP, the Model Context Protocol, are built to trust each other, the next agent obeys, and MCP servers hold credentials for each agent. Attacks a model would refuse directly can then exfiltrate database contents.

Whitney staff ratify a contract hours before their strike

Unionised workers at the Whitney Museum ratified their second contract on Sunday night, hours before a strike set for Monday, three days before previews of the Roy Lichtenstein retrospective. Every employee gets at least 14.75% more over four years. The lowest-paid get 5% to 23% in the first year, and new-hire minimums go up.
