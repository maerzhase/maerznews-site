Medicine · mRNA

A vaccine made from each patient's own tumour cuts the risk of melanoma returning, or death, by 42% in its decisive trial

Moderna and Merck released the late-stage results of intismeran, a personalised mRNA shot given alongside the immunotherapy Keytruda, two days early after a news site broke the embargo. After two years, 82% of patients on the combination were alive with no sign of the cancer coming back, against 70% on Keytruda alone.

Ars Technica · Merck · Moderna · 22 October 2026

The idea is simple to say and hard to do. Doctors sequence a patient's tumour and their healthy cells, pick out mutations that only the cancer carries, and write the code for up to 34 of them into a strand of synthetic mRNA. Injected, it teaches the immune system what that particular cancer looks like, while Keytruda, Merck's established checkpoint antibody, takes the brakes off the immune response. Every dose is made for one person.

The trial enrolled 1,137 people whose stage IIB to IV melanoma had already been surgically removed, the moment when the danger is that hidden cells seed a relapse. Two thirds got the vaccine with Keytruda, one third Keytruda with a placebo. The companies had said in August that the study had met its goal but held back the numbers for the European Society for Medical Oncology congress, which opens now in Madrid; when an oncology news site published them early, they put out the top line on Thursday. The full results have been accepted by the New England Journal of Medicine, and Merck and Moderna are already in talks with regulators.

"One of the most significant advances in melanoma treatment since the advent of immunotherapy." Dean Li, president of Merck Research Laboratories

Two cautions. The effect is smaller than in the five-year Phase 2 study, which found a 49% cut in the risk of recurrence or death and 59% for distant spread, against 42% and 38% now. And these are two-year figures from a press release, not yet the peer-reviewed paper. Still, Merck calls it the first Phase 3 study to show a clinically meaningful gain over Keytruda alone in patients whose melanoma has been surgically removed. Moderna's Stéphane Bancel calls it "a defining moment" for the technology that first reached most of us as a Covid jab.

Rail · Strike

CP conductors and ticket staff strike next Friday, and the trains through Cortegaça and Esmoriz will feel it

The SFRCI union has called a 24-hour strike of CP's conductors, ticket-office workers and their supervisors for Friday 30 October. CP warns of disruption to its services on the day, with possible knock-on effects on Thursday the 29th and Saturday the 31st, which matters on the Linha do Norte, where the Porto urban trains stop at Cortegaça, Esmoriz and Ovar.

The union's Luís Bravo says the walkout is about pay that no longer keeps up with fuel, housing, food, gas and electricity: the raises won so far, it argues, do not make up for a steady loss of purchasing power, and rising fuel costs hit rail staff hardest because their shifts start and end when no trains run. Check CP's site before travelling that week.

Brandenburg · Espionage

A suspected Russian spy is arrested in Oder-Spree, accused of scouting arms makers supplying NATO's eastern flank

Federal prosecutors, the BKA and the domestic intelligence service detained a 53-year-old Ukrainian citizen from the Russian-annexed Luhansk region on Thursday morning. He lived in Eisenhüttenstadt and, investigators believe, had worked for a Russian intelligence service since at least July 2025, spying on companies and people involved in producing tanks and ammunition for NATO's eastern flank. He is to go before an investigating judge at the Federal Court of Justice today.

It is the latest in a chain of such cases this year, and the investigators note that this one acted with specific targets and orders rather than opportunistically. Chancellor Friedrich Merz said Germany is the target of "intensive" espionage. Merz is in Brandenburg again this morning, visiting the chip researchers of the IHP institute in Frankfurt (Oder), about 25 km from where the suspect lived.

Housing · Protests

The housing movement calls marches for Saturday, as the state housing chief says the young won't make "sacrifices"

Popular assemblies in both cities approved new protests for Saturday: in Porto from 16:00 in Praça da Batalha, in Lisbon from the Marquês de Pombal, due to reach the Rossio around 19:00. DNS · Censorship

Quad9 will switch off its free DNS for France on 24 November rather than block sites for a broadcaster

A French court ruled for beIN, the Qatari sports broadcaster, ordering the Swiss non-profit resolver to stop resolving a list of piracy domains for users in France. The court cut the penalty from €580,000 to €1,000 a day, but Quad9 says the money was never the point: it treats DNS as a neutral, passive service and refuses to "lie" about the answers it gives.

Calling compliance "technically unfeasible", it will stop serving France from Tuesday 24 November. If you use 9.9.9.9 elsewhere, nothing changes, but the case is a test of how far rights-holders can push blocking down into the plumbing of the internet.

Europe · Climate

This summer's heat killed some 50,000 people in Europe's cities, the deadliest on record, and climate change caused two thirds of the deaths

Two peer-reviewed studies in Nature Health, by Imperial College London and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, modelled 854 urban areas home to about 40% of the people of the EU, Britain, Norway and Switzerland. Their estimate dwarfs the previous worst summers since 2000: about 29,000 heat deaths in 2022 and 28,000 in 2003.

Some 6,000 of this year's victims were under 65, and the south and west of Europe, Portugal's side of the continent, saw the highest temperatures. The share of deaths the authors attribute to warming keeps rising; this year it reached two in three.
