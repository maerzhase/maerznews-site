Germany · Protest · Gen Z

Germany's Gen Z takes to the streets in 34 cities: half the promised crowds by day, looting in Frankfurt by night

"Gen Z regelt" had 182,000 sign-ups and claims more than 100,000 marched. Police counted far fewer, and after dark small groups smashed shop windows in Frankfurt and fought police in Berlin.

tagesschau · rbb24 · hessenschau · BBC · 31 October – 1 November 2026

The "Gen Z regelt" ("Gen Z takes charge") campaign called protests in 34 cities for Saturday afternoon, with demands that read like a youth budget: more money for schools, addiction services and psychotherapy, action on the cost of living and climate, and no to compulsory military service or wider state surveillance. Its spokesman Amadeo spoke of "a hundred thousand people" by 8 pm. Reporters in many cities saw about half of what had been announced. In Berlin the police counted 5,000 at the Brandenburg Gate at the peak; the organisers said 11,000.

The day was peaceful; the night was not. In Frankfurt, the biggest march at about 8,000, police broke up the demonstration after firecrackers and stones, and later masked groups smashed windows on the Zeil, stole trainers and attacked a gun shop; police seized two weapons and made "numerous arrests". Hesse's interior minister Roman Poseck blamed "around 100" left-wing extremists. In Berlin a separate evening march from Rosenthaler Platz towards Prenzlauer Berg swelled to 3,500 and was broken up with water cannon. Cologne's police cancelled its march over pyrotechnics, and put the night's trouble down more to Halloween than to the protest.

"What happened after the rallies ended was beyond our control." Amadeo, spokesman for "Gen Z regelt", to dpa

The campaign says this was "only the first step" and plans to join a Europe-wide youth strike day on 20 November. Not everyone is with it: the Federal Pupils' Conference, which backs the 20 November action, keeps its distance from "Gen Z regelt", citing an opaque organising committee. The same anger filled the streets of France for a third Saturday, where the interior ministry counted 320,000 marchers and the organisers nearly a million.

Ovar · Road safety

Ovar has the second-worst record on the mainland for injury crashes on its own local roads

Off the motorways and national roads, on the streets and municipal roads the council itself runs, Ovar averaged 37.3 crashes with victims per 10,000 residents a year between 2022 and 2024. The median for Portuguese municipalities is 21.2. The ranking comes from the Transparent platform, which pulls together data from INE, the road safety authority ANSR, the insurance regulator and the public contracts portal.

Over those three years 925 people were hurt in road crashes in the municipality: four died and 39 were seriously injured. Over the decade from 2014 to 2024, 32 people died on Ovar's roads. Motorbikes are part of the picture: 2,611 were insured in Ovar in 2025, 132% more than in 2015.

Berlin · Coalition

The Left says yes: Berlin's red-green-red coalition talks start on Monday

Six weeks after the Berlin election, a special Left party conference on Saturday voted by a wide margin, with only a few votes against and abstentions, to open formal coalition talks with the Greens and the SPD. Security · Linux

A 9.8-rated hole in Linux's TCP handshake, with a root exploit written by Claude, and Ubuntu and RHEL still unpatched

CVE-2026-98432 is a use-after-free in how the kernel handles IPv4 options during the TCP handshake, reachable by any remote peer before an application sees the connection. It dates back to Linux 4.4; the fix, Eric Dumazet's switch of two kfree() calls to kfree_rcu(), shipped in the stable kernels on 11 and 15 October. Anthropic, whose researcher reported it, says Claude wrote a working proof-of-concept that gets root on servers with a multipath TCP listener, and Go programs since 1.24 open those by default.

Debian, SUSE, Oracle's UEK and Amazon Linux have fixed kernels; AlmaLinux has them in testing. Ubuntu and RHEL did not as of Saturday evening, and Red Hat raised the bug to Critical on Friday. Until then: drop incoming TCP packets that carry IPv4 options (an nftables rule matching ip hdrlength gt 5), turn off MPTCP if unused, and reboot once patched; containers inherit the host kernel.

Who Killed Alex Odeh? (2026): a 41-year-old "unsolved" bombing that was solved within days

Documentary · dir. Jason Osder & William Youmans · IMDb 7.3 · RT 67%

On 11 October 1985 a bomb at the California office of the American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee killed its West Coast director, Alex Odeh, leaving his wife Norma and three young daughters. The film shows the case was effectively cracked within days: the three main suspects, all members of the far-right Jewish Defense League, were able to flee to Israel, and four decades on no one has been prosecuted or extradited.

Osder, who is Jewish, and Youmans, a Palestinian-American, borrow the look of true crime but, as IndieWire's critic puts it, give shape to the absence of a mystery. "Scattered but searing", says the review.

Italy · Art crime

Italy's art police let an algorithm hunt for stolen works, and it has found $200 million worth

Since 2023 the Carabinieri's cultural heritage squad has used SWOADS, the Stolen Works of Art Detection System built by its own IT engineer Salvatore Rapicavoli. At the Mercanteinfiera antiques fair in October 2024, officers photographed stands and the system flagged a portrait of a young woman with a scroll: a close, though not exact, match for a 17th-century Saint Agnes taken 20 years earlier from the small church of Virgo Potens in Sulmona, Abruzzo.

The painting is back in its church; the thieves have not been found. In all, SWOADS has helped recover more than 300 large paintings and statues and over 165,000 small objects such as coins, letters and medals.

SCTV: The Greatest Show You've Never Heard Of (2026): the Canadian sketch show that made John Candy, Rick Moranis and Catherine O'Hara

Documentary · comedy history · Netflix · since Fri 30 Oct · IMDb 7.8

Second City Television ran from 1976 to 1984, sending up commercials, films and talk shows, and launched Eugene Levy, Andrea Martin, Martin Short and Dave Thomas, alongside the late Joe Flaherty. Director Jason Hehir tells the whole arc: the rivalry with "SNL", the rescue by Edmonton billionaire Charles Allard, Bob and Doug McKenzie's breakout, and the move to NBC, where 30 minutes became 90 and network meddling drove the cast away.

Its centrepiece is a couch session filmed in July 2025 with Short, O'Hara, Levy, Thomas and Martin watching old clips, six months before O'Hara died. What's on Netflix calls it short, sweet and earnest. A Netflix original, so it is streaming in Portugal and Germany too.

Poland · Rule of law

Poland's president shoulders his way into the besieged constitutional court, and Tusk calls it an attack on democracy

The standoff over Poland's constitutional court escalated overnight into Saturday. In a video posted by one of its judges, President Karol Nawrocki, a former amateur boxer, is seen forcing his way into the court building, ringed by protesters and police, and pushing a judge down as he goes. The government had earlier suspended the court's president and vice-president.

Prime Minister Donald Tusk answered in a television address: the rule of law differs "from the law of the jungle" in that muscle and impudence do not decide who is right, and for years organisations and politicians in Poland have wanted to destroy democracy. The national-conservative opposition PiS, through Mateusz Morawiecki, has announced a motion of no confidence in his government.
