Technology · Spaceflight · Starship Flight 15

Caught by the chopsticks: after 14 orbits and 22 hours in space, SpaceX's Starship flies home and is snatched out of the air by its launch tower

The first time any rocket's upper stage has been recovered this way after an orbital flight, and it worked at the first attempt. The booster was not so lucky: it was destroyed over the Gulf of Mexico, so the fully reusable rocket is still half a step away.

Ars Technica · heise online · Reuters · BBC · as read at 07:00 on 15 November 2026

Starship launched from Starbase in South Texas on Friday on the programme's fifteenth test flight, the fourth this year. The upper stage, the ship, went into orbit, released 26 of SpaceX's new third-generation Starlink satellites and circled the planet fourteen times. About 22 hours and 32 minutes after lift-off it came back through the atmosphere, fell belly-first towards Texas, then lit three Raptor engines at the last moment, swung upright and eased down beside the tower, where two arms closed on a pair of pins each sticking out barely a foot from its hull.

The first half of the plan had gone wrong. The Super Heavy booster, which SpaceX has caught before, was supposed to return to the tower too, but a low-propellant alarm triggered and it was destroyed by its flight-termination system over the Gulf of Mexico. Had both stages come home, SpaceX would have recovered a complete orbital rocket for the first time. What state the caught ship is in is not yet known.

SpaceX has now returned both elements of a Starship rocket safely to its launch tower. Ars Technica, reporting from Starbase

Why it matters: catching the ship is what turns Starship from an expendable heavy lifter into the rapidly reusable system its whole economics depend on. Ars Technica notes the Space Shuttle flew 135 times in 30 years; SpaceX wants 135 flights a year. Its factory now turns out a ship every three weeks and was sized for one every three days. Starship is also the lander NASA has booked to put astronauts back on the Moon, and the only rocket big enough for Starlink's next generation. The hardest part, a heat shield that survives re-entry without weeks of repair, is still ahead.

Válega · Architecture · Books

Born in Válega, built across Portugal, barely studied: Januário Godinho, Ovar's modernist, finally gets a book of his own

"Januário Godinho – Arquitecto Moderno", by the architect and professor Domingos Tavares, is the first monograph devoted entirely to the man OvarNews calls one of the most productive Portuguese architects of the twentieth century. Godinho was born in Válega in 1910, trained at the Porto School of Fine Arts and died in Porto in 1990, leaving buildings in dozens of towns, yet his work has remained little studied.

He started out in the 1930s, when modernism and the classical tradition were pulling against each other, worked on Porto's urban and industrial transformation, drew the urban plans for Caramulo and Felgueiras in the 1940s and took part in the Cávado hydroelectric works. Courthouses, markets, spas, research centres and factories followed, from Chaves and Covilhã to Tomar and Vila Nova de Famalicão. The book is launched on 24 November at 18:30 in the Casa Comum, Sala do Fundo Antigo, in Porto (see the Diary).

SPD · Leadership

Hubertus Heil is the first to declare for the SPD leadership, and his letter is a pointed message to Lars Klingbeil

The former labour minister has told the party's state branches and executive in a letter, seen by the ARD's Berlin studio, that he will stand as one of the SPD's two co-leaders at the federal party conference in Bremen in mid-January, when the whole leadership is re-elected. Read in Berlin as a dig at Klingbeil, who is party leader, finance minister and vice-chancellor at once, it reopens the SPD's old argument over whether party and government posts should be split, as they were under Olaf Scholz, when Saskia Esken and Klingbeil ran the party from outside the cabinet. Housing · Protest

Sixty thousand or eight thousand? Housing marches fill more than thirty cities, and Lisbon's camp moves to the Restauradores

Saturday's national day of protest, called by the Casa para Viver platform and swelled by the CGTP union federation, Movimento Vida Justa and the Acampada de Lisboa, brought about 100,000 people onto the streets of more than thirty towns and cities, by the organisers' count. In Lisbon the march ran from the Alameda down the Avenida Almirante Reis to the Martim Moniz, which filled while most marchers were still on the avenue: 60,000, said the organisers; 8,000, said the police.

The marchers want lower rents, more protection for tenants and a halt to the government's changes to the rental law. In Porto thousands walked from the Marquês to the Praça D. João I through rain that turned heavy near the end. Overnight the Lisbon acampada, the protest camp that has occupied the Rossio for a month, voted to pitch its tents in the Restauradores instead; the PSP says it will take a "preventive posture". Next dates: a CGTP demonstration in Lisbon on 21 November and another Casa para Viver march on 12 December.

Security · Vibe coding

Passport numbers and 200,000 private messages: researchers find thousands of apps built with Lovable leaking their databases

The security researchers at Crissy Field, working with Handelsblatt, traced 159,041 projects built on the Swedish AI app-builder Lovable through the Common Crawl archive. 34,545 used a Supabase database, and 24,664 of those sent database records to anyone who loaded the page. With a few more read-only requests, the researchers found tables holding e-mail addresses in 7,403 of those sites, about 30%.

The worst cases: a German lifestyle forum exposing more than 80,000 profiles with names, e-mails and birth dates plus over 200,000 chat messages; a service provider leaking more than 1,000 identity-document records with passport numbers; one site showing 280,000 e-mails sent to its customers. Lovable markets itself as needing no technical knowledge, but its terms leave data security to the customer.

New York · Restitution

A $180m Van Gogh goes under the hammer on Wednesday, and the heirs of its German publisher-owner say it was sold under Nazi duress

Sotheby's is due to sell Châtaigniers en fleurs (Blossoming Chestnut Trees) in New York on Wednesday as part of the Blaquier-Arrieta Collection, with an estimate of $180m. Representatives of the heirs of the Fischer family, its earlier German owners, say the painting may have been sold under duress as the Nazis rose to power. Samuel Fischer was a famous German publisher.

Sotheby's says its own "extensive research" into the work's ownership since 1890 has not established a forced sale, but that it takes restitution "extremely seriously" and will work with the family towards "a mutually satisfactory resolution". Whether the sale goes ahead as planned is the question for the week.

London · Portuguese song

Ten Portuguese women in London sing the songs of the mondadeiras, at the London Jazz Festival today, the same day Mariza plays

Traditional polyphony · a cappella choir · London Jazz Festival · Sunday 15 Nov

Amarílis, a choir of ten emigrant women aged 33 to 39, most with no musical background, was founded about a year ago by Inês Loubet, an academic who has lived in London since 2013 and co-founded Porto's Sopa de Pedra collective in 2011. Its aim is to show that Portugal's female polyphonic singing deserves the attention the mostly male cante alentejano gets.

The repertoire runs from cante adaptations to Azorean ballads, Madeiran cantigas, songs from Trás-os-Montes and from the Beira Baixa singer Catarina Chitas: "Cantiga da monda", "Mondadeiras lindo rancho", "Já os Galos Cantam", "Senhora da Nazaré". Loubet picked songs that women sing in the first person, she told Lusa, because so few exist.

Ukraine · Diplomacy

Donbas for NATO? Zelensky says US envoys floated the trade for the first time, and he would put a real offer to a referendum
