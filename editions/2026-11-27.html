Technology · Jobs · Walldorf

SAP draws up plans to cut at least 10,000 jobs to put AI first: almost one in ten of its staff

Every department of Europe's most valuable software company has been told to say which posts can go, Bloomberg reports, with programming, product support and sales first in line. SAP does not deny it.

heise online · Bloomberg · Reuters

SAP is working out how to shed at least 10,000 employees as part of an "AI first" strategy, Bloomberg reported on Thursday, citing people familiar with the plans. The company did not deny it and spoke of "continuous reviews of the organisation". Ten thousand is close to a tenth of the 110,730 people SAP employed at the end of September. Every department has been told to identify posts it can cut, because management wants to change how SAP writes and sells software; the jobs most at risk are in programming, product support and sales.

It would be the second round in three years. In 2024 SAP cut 10,000 jobs, mostly through voluntary departures, and has since added about 15,000 again. A German works agreement on job security runs out at the end of this year. In September, chief executive Christian Klein said "the chance that SAP will outgrow itself once more has never been greater", and signed a new agreement with the works council of the German parent company that some media wrongly described as a job guarantee until the end of 2028.

"A price tag for dismissals, and no protection beyond the statutory protection against dismissal."IG Metall, on SAP's new works agreement

The union IG Metall says that agreement no longer gives keeping jobs "top priority" and speaks only of "socially responsible support for processes of change". It contains no explicit exclusion of compulsory redundancies, it says, and mainly sets the level of severance pay; SAP refused social criteria for choosing who goes, and binding deadlines for offering a new post.

The report landed the same week that SAP's home industry agreed its pay deal (see Germany), and as the rest of the sector argues about what AI coding tools do to headcount. For the people who build software, the point is plain: at Walldorf, the jobs named first are the ones that write the code.

Maceda · Air Force

Four new Black Hawks at Maceda: the air base next door turns three

Base Aérea n.º 8 in Maceda, the parish just south of Cortegaça, marked its third anniversary on Tuesday with a ceremony led by the Air Force's vice chief of staff, Lieutenant-General João Cardeiro Caldas. Its commander, Colonel Fernando Rocha, listed the year's gains: four new UH-60L Black Hawk helicopters, more operational capacity and 239 training courses for its people.

The base is home to Esquadra 551, the "Panteras", which flies the Black Hawks and spent much of the year fighting rural fires, and to a permanent detachment of Esquadra 552, the "Zangões", whose AW119 Koala helicopters fly search and rescue and medical evacuations, mostly for the north of the country. The ceremony ended with a tribute to the military and civilian staff who have died and with decorations; Rocha also pointed to joint work with Ovar's council and the parishes around the base.

Germany · Berlin & Brandenburg

Pay · Metal & electrical industry

4.3 per cent in two steps: industry's pilot deal ends the hardest warning strikes in years

IG Metall and the employers settled in Mannheim after a fourth round for Baden-Württemberg and North Rhine-Westphalia that ran for more than 24 hours. Pay rises 2.0 per cent on 1 May 2027 and another 2.3 per cent on 1 April 2028, with a one-off payment of up to €450; the deal runs 25 months. As a pilot agreement it can be taken over for all 3.7 million workers in the industry. More than 950,000 people joined the warning strikes before it, the union says.

The price was flexibility: for the first time the union accepted optional departures from the 35-hour week. Unpaid extra hours and pay cuts become possible for a limited time to back investments, capped at eight per cent of a worker's agreed pay. The split in the industry made the talks hard, with carmakers and suppliers weak while aerospace, medical technology, electrical engineering and defence boom. Not everyone is at peace: Mercedes-Benz wants lower labour costs and an end to the 35-hour week, and Volkswagen's in-house talks for about 120,000 staff began this week.

Environment · Waste

Nineteen landfills broke the law last year, burying 1.4 million tonnes of rubbish untreated

The environmental group Zero found it in the state's own figures: according to the 2025 annual urban-waste report of the Agência Portuguesa do Ambiente, at least 19 landfills for household waste took in 1.4 million tonnes without the pre-treatment the law requires. Environment minister Maria da Graça Carvalho has asked the environmental inspectorate, IGAMAOT, to find out "why this is happening", and the agency for a plan to bring the sites back within the law.

The APA answered within hours that licensing and inspecting the landfills is the job of the regional coordination commissions, the CCDR, and of IGAMAOT, and that it was the APA itself that found the breaches, reported them and published the data. It says untreated waste sent to landfill fell ten per cent up to 2025, and that it is working with the CCDRs, inter-municipal communities and councils on more separate collection and treatment capacity.

Hardware · Memory crisis

The memory crunch reaches your next phone: makers cut their 2027 targets by up to 29 per cent

High memory prices are making phone makers plan for fewer sales, says a report by Jiemian News from supply-chain sources. Vivo drops from 85 to 60 million units (−29%), Xiaomi from 120 to 90 million (−25%), Motorola from 50 to 40 million and Oppo from 105 to 90 million. Apple trims 233 to 220 million and Samsung about 232 to 210 million; only Honor holds at 50 million.

Counterpoint counts 19 per cent fewer phones shipped in the third quarter than a year earlier, the fewest in 14 years, and expects memory to stay dear because big new fabs will not open before 2028. Goldman Sachs puts the DRAM shortfall at 5.1 per cent next year, one of the worst in 15 years. On Hacker News, the same squeeze has reached hard drives.

Portugal · Cinemas

Forty screens may go dark: the government approves nine cinema closures at once

The culture ministry has granted nine requests, pending since 2024, to take 40 screens out of cinema use, the inspectorate IGAC told Lusa. Most are in shopping centres: Maia Shopping and Gaia Shopping near Porto, Nova Arcada in Braga, Rio Sul in Seixal, LoureShopping and La Vie in Guarda, where three of four screens had already become a Normal store without permission and the owner faces a fine. Five more requests are still open, from Algarve Shopping to Caldas da Rainha, where three screens were knocked down unasked for shops and a gym. Since 2025 Cineplace has gone bust and NOS has closed rooms; 541 screens were working in October. "Closing a cinema is not the same as closing a shoe shop," minister Margarida Balseiro Lopes said in July.

Porto · Serralves

Anri Sala's first big show in Portugal turns Siza's museum into an instrument

Fallen Vision opens tonight at the Museu de Serralves and runs until 2 May. The Albanian-born artist, who represented France at the 2013 Venice Biennale, has spent almost thirty years crossing film, music and architecture. The show is built as a single installation, with works from across his career, some newly configured, and a large selection of the frescoes he has been painting in Naples, a return to the technique he studied in Tirana in the 1990s, before video.

Its soundtrack is Surface, Otherwise (After STA XLII), a new choral piece written with the composer Jack Sheen from the day-sections of plaster of one fresco. It makes the light pulse in several rooms and veils and unveils the windows onto the park, and quietly quotes his Messiaen film Time No Longer (2021). Inês Grosso curates; entry to tonight's opening is free.

EU · Budget

Merz calls off day two: EU leaders fail to agree a €1.7 trillion budget for 2028–2034

The special summit in Brussels was meant to last two days. On Thursday night Friedrich Merz and Emmanuel Macron came out before the official end to say there would be no deal, and the 27 leaders dropped Friday's session. The last proposal was about €1,700 billion over seven years for farming, the economy, defence and the regions; Germany wants several hundred billion less, rejects the latest compromise, ties its consent to aid for Ukraine and rules out new EU debt, warning that interest will already cost "over €150 billion" in those seven years.

Merz met the net payers first, the Netherlands, Austria, Sweden, Finland, Denmark and Belgium, a group that now calls itself "the sensible seven". On the other side, Slovakia's Robert Fico, with Poland, Hungary and Czechia, threatens to block the plans, the Ukraine money above all. Council president António Costa calls the summit a necessary step towards agreement in December; Macron says it is December or 2027.
